The Underground Comix Chronicles

— tracing the uncensored history of the comic rebellion

Warning: Some episodes discuss NSFW content with academic rigor—explaining satirical intent while acknowledging historical harm. We don't sanitize history; we contextualize it.

👋 Welcome to the Trail

This series digs into the raw, unvarnished truth of the 1960s-70s underground comix revolution. Artists like R. Crumb, Gilbert Shelton, S. Clay Wilson, and Trina Robbins menaced the mainstream Comics Code Authority with pen and ink attacks on American hypocrisy.

🔥 Key Figures

R. Crumb

The nervous wreck with a pen. Created Fritz the Cat, Keep on Truckin', and a lifetime of obscenity cases. Obsessed with the 'sinister quality' of American life.

Gilbert Shelton

Texas transplant who created the Freak Brothers. Eternal stoner who lived to see his 80s while drawing wise guys eternally young.

S. Clay Wilson

Master of density and gore. Used intricate cross-hatching to shock the bourgeoisie. Captain Piss-Gums was pure revolutionary fury.

Trina Robbins

The feminist who challenged the boy's club. Created Wimmen's Comix, drew the first lesbian comic, became Wonder Woman's savior. Called a 'feminazi' for having standards.

"The underground comix movement was a grenade with the safety pin pulled, and we were all standing in the blast radius of mainstream decency."
— The Ink-Stained Freak, 1971

Zap Comix: The Revolutionary Freak Show

#1

Spring 1968 — The Launch

Charles Plymell's Print Mint crew printed just 3,500 copies at 25¢ each. The cover featured a nude human figure in electric fetal position, plugged into an otherworldly cord. Tagline: "The comic that plugs you in!!"

#4

Winter 1968 — Where It Got Real

Crumb's 'Joe Blow' saga—an incest fantasy that savaged nuclear family values as thoroughly as it titillated readers. The suburbia depicted was a nightmarish parody of 1950s conformity, complete with dysfunctional families in grotesque sexual rituals.

🎭 Key Stories in Zap #4

  • Joe Blow — The incest protagonist tramping through a dystopian American landscape
  • The Snoid — Crumb's gnome-like creatures existing in LSD's shadow realm
  • Red Hotspot — A psychedelic journey through drugged consciousness

💰 Distribution

The Comix Code was dead. Zap sold for 25¢ (half mainstream price). Distributor Charlie Benderman smuggled issues through head shops and hippie dumpsters. The feds were watching, but the underground was built for evasion.

Wimmen's Comix: The Feminist Revolution

The Boy's Club Bustup

When Trina Robbins started objecting to rampant misogyny in Zap and other underground comix, the response was swift. Crumb and gang called her a "feminazi" with "no sense of humor." They drew women being humiliated and raped in graphic detail and called it 'satire.'

The Schism (1970)

While the Zap crew attracted titty bar crowds and college stoner conventions, Robbins, Mary Fleener, and others said "enough." They launched Wimmen's Comix—the first all-women comix anthology that dared to show women as agents of narrative, not just victims of male fantasy.

Trina Robbins

Drew the first lesbian comic in underground comix history. Later became Wonder Woman's first female artist.

Mary Fleener

Created 'Teenage Heroin Queen,' challenging female addiction narratives with raw honesty.

Karen Green

NYSAC president who also published comics under various imprints. Navigated both politics and the underground scene.

"They called me a feminist zealot. But I was just asking: how come when we draw ourselves, it's 'edgy,' but when they draw us tortured, it's 'art'?"
— Trina Robbins, 1972

The Backlash

Even the movement that claimed to fight oppression had blind spots. Robbins paid the price for her principles. Underground comix celebrated freedom—except when that freedom meant women controlling their own narratives.

📚 Key Contributions

  • Wimmen's Comix #1 (1972) — debut issue, all-women contributors
  • It Ain't Me, Babe (Robbins' earlier feminist zine, 1970)
  • Fridays (1976) — continuation of the all-women comix line

Legal Battles: When the Courts Got Freaky

🚨 People v. Kirkpatrick (1970)

Charles Kirkpatrick, publisher of Zap #4, was arrested after selling copies to undercover NYPD officers. Charge: distribution of obscene materials.

The defense called comic legend Gil Kane as an expert witness. The NY Court of Appeals didn't care—conviction upheld.

1970

People v. Kirkpatrick

NY Court of Appeals upholds Zap #4 conviction. Defense expert Gil Kane testifies but loses anyway. Zap distribution crippled in New York.

1972

Gay News v. Gotch

California Supreme Court ruled police couldn't seize publications merely for selling to undercover buyers—entrapment occurred. A legal crack in the obscenity wall.

1973

Miller v. California

Supreme Court's "community standards" test destroyed distribution networks. Local authorities could claim any comic was obscene under their definition. End of an era.
