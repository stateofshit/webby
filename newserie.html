🔥 Key Figures

R. Crumb

The nervous wreck with a pen. Created Fritz the Cat, Keep on Truckin', and a lifetime of obscenity cases. Obsessed with the 'sinister quality' of American life.

Gilbert Shelton

Texas transplant who created the Freak Brothers. Eternal stoner who lived to see his 80s while drawing wise guys eternally young.

S. Clay Wilson

Master of density and gore. Used intricate cross-hatching to shock the bourgeoisie. His Captain Piss-Gums was pure revolutionary fury.

Trina Robbins

The feminist who challenged the boy's club. Created Wimmen's Comix, drew first lesbian comic, became Wonder Woman's savior. Called a 'feminazi' for having standards.

Zap Comix: The Revolutionary Freak Show

📚 Zap #1 (Spring 1968)

With only 3,500 copies printed by Charles Plymell, Zap launched from San Francisco's legendary Print Mint. The cover featured the seminal image that would define underground comix forever—a nude human figure in electric fetal position, plugged into an otherworldly umbilical cord. The tagline? “The comic that plugs you in!!”

📚 Zap #4 (Winter 1968)

Holy shit, man. This is where it got real. Crumb's 'Joe Blow' saga—an incest fantasy that savaged the nuclear family as thoroughly as it titillated the base instincts of readers. The suburbia depicted was a nightmarish parody of 1950s conformity, complete with dysfunctional families engaged in grotesque sexual rituals.

🎭 Key Stories in Zap #4

  • Joe Blow - The incest protagonist who tramps through a dystopian American landscape, raping and being raped in equal measure
  • The Snoid - Crumb's perverse gnome-like creatures that existed in LSD's shadow realm
  • Red Hotspot - A psychedelic journey through drugged consciousness

💰 Distribution

Look, the Comix Code was dead as a doornail. Zap sold for 25¢ (half the cover price of Comics Code-approved titles). Distributor Charlie Benderman smuggled issues through head shops and hippie dumpsters. The feds were watching, but the underground was built for evasion.

Wimmen's Comix: The Feminazi Revolution

The Boy's Club Bustup

When Trina Robbins started objecting to the rampant misogyny in Zap and other underground comix, the response was swift and brutal. Crumb and gang called her a 'feminazi' with 'no sense of humor'. They drew women being humiliated and raped in graphic detail and called it 'satire.'

The Schism

1970 marked the fracture line. While the Zap crew attracted titty bar crowds and college stoner conventions, Robbins and other women like Trina, Mary Fleener, and ografia said "enough." They launched Wimmen's Comix—the first all-women comix anthology that dared to show women as agents of narrative, not just victims of male fantasy.

📚 Key Contributions

  • Trina Robbins - Drew the first lesbian comic in underground comix history
  • Mary Fleener - Created the 'Teenage Heroin Queen,' challenging female addiction narratives
  • Victoria Helen Weston - Wrote 'The House of Ugly Sex' deconstructing male fantasy

The Backlash

Even the movement that claimed to fight oppression had its blind spots. Robbins, who later became the first woman to draw Wonder Woman, paid the price for her principles. Underground comix celebrated freedom—except when that freedom meant women controlling their own narratives.

“They called me a feminist, a feminist zealot. But I was just asking: how come when we draw ourselves, it's 'edgy,' but when they draw us tortured, it's 'art'?”
— Trina Robbins, 1972

Legal Battles: When the Courts Got Freaky

🚨 People v. Kirkpatrick (1970)

This case nearly killed the underground comix scene. Charles Kirkpatrick, publisher of Zap #4, was arrested after selling copies to undercover NYPD officers. The charge? Distribution of obscene materials.

Here's the kicker—the defense called comic book legend Gil Kane as an expert witness to testify that Zap #4 was "obscene in nature but not obscene in content." The NY Court of Appeals didn't care. They upheld the conviction anyway.

⚖️ The Gotch Solution

In *Gay News v. Gotch* (1972), the California Supreme Court ruled that police couldn't seize publications merely for selling to undercover buyers—entrapment had occurred. This created a legal crack in the obscenity wall that underground publishers exploited for years.

💣 Miller v. California (1973)

The Supreme Court dropped the Hammer of Miller, establishing the "community standards" test for obscenity. This meant local authorities could now claim any comic they disliked was obscene under their community's definition of decency. Distribution networks died as distributors feared prosecution.
